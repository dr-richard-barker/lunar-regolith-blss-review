\section{Multiphase Fluid Dynamics and Root Zone Aeration in Lunar Gravity}
\label{sec:fluid_dynamics}

\subsection{Fractional Gravity and Capillary Dominance}
\label{subsec:fractional_gravity}

Fluid dynamics in terrestrial soils reflect a dynamic equilibrium between gravitational drainage and capillary matric suction\cite{steinberg2007fluid,jones1999microgravity}. On the lunar surface, gravitational acceleration drops to $g_L \approx 1.62\,\mathrm{m/s}^2$, approximately one-sixth of terrestrial gravity ($g_E \approx 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s}^2$)\cite{ming1989lunar}. This reduction fundamentally alters the dimensionless Bond number ($\mathrm{Bo}$), which defines the ratio of gravitational body forces to interfacial capillary surface tension forces within a porous medium\cite{ming1989lunar,ostrach1982low}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Bo} = \frac{\Delta\rho \cdot g \cdot d^2}{\gamma}
    \label{eq:bond_number}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta\rho$ is the density difference between the liquid nutrient solution and the habitat atmosphere ($\mathrm{kg/m}^3$), $g$ is the local gravitational acceleration ($\mathrm{m/s}^2$), $d$ is the characteristic pore-throat diameter ($\mathrm{m}$), and $\gamma$ is the liquid-gas surface tension ($\mathrm{N/m}$)\cite{ming1989lunar,jones1999microgravity}.

Because lunar gravity is reduced by a factor of 6 while fluid surface tension remains intrinsically fluid-dependent, the Bond number decreases by nearly an order of magnitude\cite{ming1989lunar,ostrach1982low,podolskiy2005capillary}. Consequently, the hydraulic regime transitions from gravity-driven drainage to capillary-dominated retention\cite{ming1989lunar,podolskiy2005capillary}. The characteristic length scale governing this balance is the capillary length ($L_c$):
\begin{equation}
    L_c = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{\rho g}}
    \label{eq:capillary_length}
\end{equation}
For pure bulk water ($\gamma \approx 0.072\,\mathrm{N/m}$, $\rho = 1000\,\mathrm{kg/m}^3$), the theoretical capillary length expands from $2.71\,\mathrm{mm}$ on Earth to approximately $6.5\text{--}6.6\,\mathrm{mm}$ under lunar gravity ($1.62\text{--}1.76\,\mathrm{m/s}^2$). Correspondingly, the classical Young-Laplace equilibrium height of capillary rise ($h_c$):
\begin{equation}
    h_c = \frac{2\gamma \cos\theta}{\rho g r}
    \label{eq:capillary_rise}
\end{equation}
(where $\theta$ is the fluid contact angle and $r$ is the effective pore radius) predicts a six-fold vertical expansion of the capillary fringe compared to Earth for identical pore geometries\cite{ming1989lunar,steinberg2007fluid}.

\subsection{Empirical Suborbital Flight Validation: Microscale Topography and Film Thickening}
\label{subsec:empirical_flight_validation}

While classical continuum models assume idealized smooth surfaces and unconstrained bulk fluid properties, empirical data gathered aboard Blue Origin's New Shepard 29 (NS-29) suborbital mission by Hasenstein, Kiss, and McKay (2026) demonstrates that boundary interactions in fractional gravity diverge markedly from textbook theory\cite{hasenstein2026assessing}. Conducting flight experiments under sustained centrifugal lunar acceleration ($a = 1.76 \pm 0.02\,\mathrm{m/s}^2$ for $2.3\,\mathrm{min}$), Hasenstein et al. imaged fluid menisci across pure water, $30\%$ (w/w) glycerol ($\eta = 2.5\,\mathrm{cP}$), and $0.8\%$ (w/w) saline solutions in 3D-printed chambers with wall angles of $90^\circ, 75^\circ$, and $70^\circ$, fitting interface geometries using the nonlinear Young--Laplace differential formulation\cite{hasenstein2026assessing}:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d^2z}{dx^2} = \frac{\rho g}{\gamma}(z - z_0) \left[ 1 + \left(\frac{dz}{dx}\right)^2 \right]^{3/2}
    \label{eq:young_laplace_diff}
\end{equation}
with the wall slope constrained by the apparent interface contact angle $\theta_\mathrm{app} = \theta_\mathrm{wall} - \theta_\mathrm{contact}$ through $dz/dx = \tan(\theta_\mathrm{app})$.

The empirical capillary length measured for water under lunar gravity was $L_c = 4.2 \pm 0.1\,\mathrm{mm}$ (yielding an apparent fitted surface tension $\gamma \approx 30.0\text{--}30.6\,\mathrm{mN/m}$), representing a $\sim 35\%$ suppression relative to theoretical bulk values ($6.5\,\mathrm{mm}$)\cite{hasenstein2026assessing}. Hasenstein et al. established that in reduced gravity, microscale surface topography, wall roughness, and contact angle hysteresis override bulk fluid behavior\cite{hasenstein2026assessing}. Fluids form thicker wetting films along container boundaries, while menisci exhibit pronounced curvature and flatter central zones compared to terrestrial gravity\cite{hasenstein2026assessing}. Adding $30\%$ glycerol increased apparent surface tension ($\gamma \approx 43.9\text{--}50.1\,\mathrm{mN/m}$) and expanded capillary length to $L_c = 4.8\text{--}5.2 \pm 0.9\,\mathrm{mm}$ across all wall angles\cite{hasenstein2026assessing}. Biocompatible wetting agents can therefore overcome microscale boundary pinning and improve fluid wicking along textured porous surfaces in fractional gravity\cite{hasenstein2026assessing}.

\subsection{The Raw Regolith Hydrology Dilemma vs. Sintered Pore Architecture}
\label{subsec:pore_engineering}

These empirical flight findings illuminate the severe, multifaceted hydraulic failure modes of raw lunar regolith farming. Natural lunar regolith is intrinsically hydrophobic at the individual particle scale, as recently demonstrated by single-particle contact angle analyses on returned Chang'e-5 grains\cite{zhang2025wettability}. In unsorted, polydisperse regolith beds, sub-micrometer dust particles create an uncontrolled, chaotic pore network spanning from $<0.1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ to $>250\,\mu\mathrm{m}$\cite{paul2022plants,turci2015mechanistic}. Under $1/6\,g$, capillary forces trap water in fine micropores ($<10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$), establishing an expansive saturated zone that suffocates aerobic root respiration\cite{paul2022plants,ming1989lunar,stout2001evidence}. Simultaneously, the combined effects of dust hydrophobicity, low gravitational driving head, and contact-angle pinning severely impede water percolation, leading to erratic bypass channels and dry zones\cite{hasenstein2026assessing,zhang2025wettability}. 

To prevent hypoxia in fractional gravity, microgravity plant cultivation systems (such as NASA's Veggie and Advanced Plant Habitat) rely on coarse, sieved aggregates ($1\text{--}2\,\mathrm{mm}$ arcillite)\cite{hasenstein2026assessing,stout2001evidence,steinberg2007fluid}. Hasenstein et al. estimate that an aggregate diameter of $0.5\text{--}1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ would be required to maintain aeration under lunar gravity\cite{hasenstein2026assessing}. However, Apollo core stratigraphy\cite{mckay1991lunar} and Chang'e-5 drill core depth profiles\cite{zhao2023variations} demonstrate that millimeter-scale regolith fragments dominate only at depths of several meters, whereas surface regolith consists predominantly of fine, sub-$75\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ dust. Sourcing coarse regolith on the Moon therefore requires extensive deep excavation and heavy sifting plants, imposing prohibitive launch-mass and energy burdens on early outposts\cite{hasenstein2026assessing}.

Engineered thermal sintering resolves this dilemma by transforming the abundant, hazardous fine surface dust ($45\text{--}75\,\mu\mathrm{m}$) into structured porous ceramic matrices with monodisperse pore throat diameters concentrated precisely between $30$ and $60\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, and open porosities of $35\text{--}42\%$\cite{ming1989lunar}. High-temperature processing ($>1050^\circ\mathrm{C}$) destroys surface organic/radiation defect layers, rounds angular particle edges, and vitrifies the aluminosilicate matrix into an intrinsically hydrophilic substrate\cite{ming1989lunar,taylor2005microwave,xi2026multiphysics}.

The resulting pore network stabilizes the substrate's soil-water retention curve, formulated via the van Genuchten constitutive relation\cite{jones2002liquid,heinse2007measurement,jones2012beyond}:
\begin{equation}
    \Theta(h) = \frac{\theta(h) - \theta_r}{\theta_s - \theta_r} = \left[ 1 + \left(\alpha |h|\right)^n \right]^{-m}
    \label{eq:van_genuchten}
\end{equation}
where $\Theta(h)$ is normalized volumetric water content, $\theta(h)$ is volumetric moisture content, $\theta_r$ and $\theta_s$ are residual and saturated moisture contents, $h$ is matric suction head ($\mathrm{cm}$ or $\mathrm{kPa}$), and $\alpha, n$, and $m = 1 - 1/n$ are empirical pore-geometry parameters\cite{jones2002liquid,heinse2007measurement,jones2012beyond}. Using the simplified Arya--Paris particle-to-pore scaling relation ($r = R \left[\frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{\phi}{1-\phi}\right)\right]^{0.5}$, where $R$ is mean particle radius and $\phi$ is porosity)\cite{arya1981physicoempirical,jones2012beyond} together with Miller--Miller similarity scaling ($\alpha_g = \alpha_{1g} \cdot g/g_E$)\cite{miller1955theory,jones2012beyond}, Jones et al. (2012) demonstrated a sixfold vertical expansion of the equilibrium capillary fringe in lunar regolith ($d_\mathrm{mean} = 0.072\,\mathrm{mm}$\cite{carrier2003particle}): $50\%$ relative saturation occurs $400\,\mathrm{cm}$ ($4\,\mathrm{m}$) above a free water table on the Moon versus $70\,\mathrm{cm}$ on Earth\cite{jones2012beyond}. Passive gravitational drainage alone is therefore physically infeasible in raw lunar dust without prohibitively tall root columns\cite{jones2012beyond}.

Moreover, adequate root-zone gas exchange requires maintaining $10\text{--}20\%$ air-filled porosity ($\varepsilon = \phi - \theta$)\cite{bunt1988physical,jones2012beyond} and keeping relative gas diffusivity ($D_p/D_o$) above the critical percolation threshold of $0.02$ ($2\%$)\cite{schjonning2003linking,jones2012beyond}, as quantified by Moldrup et al.'s Water-induced Linear Reduction (WLR) model\cite{moldrup2000predicting,jones2012beyond}:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{D_p}{D_o} = \frac{\varepsilon^{2.5}}{\phi}
    \label{eq:wlr_diffusivity}
\end{equation}
For saturated raw lunar regolith fines ($\theta = 0.41$, $\phi = 0.45$), Eq.~\eqref{eq:wlr_diffusivity} yields $D_p/D_o \approx 0.0007$, well below the $0.02$ aerobic threshold. By eliminating both ultra-fine micropores ($<5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$) that trigger capillary waterlogging and macropores ($>150\,\mu\mathrm{m}$) that cause premature draining, sintered ceramics constrain matric suction to an optimal operating window of $-2.5$ to $-5.0\,\mathrm{kPa}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:hydrodynamics_aeration} and Table~\ref{tab:hydraulic_properties})\cite{ming1989lunar}. This suction is sufficient to wick nutrient solutions across the root zone against fractional gravity, even when factoring in boundary film thickening and micro-roughness pinning\cite{hasenstein2026assessing}, while allowing plant roots to extract water with minimal metabolic energy expenditure\cite{ming1989lunar}. Simultaneously, this pore architecture maintains air-filled porosity above $25\text{--}30\%$ at field capacity under $1/6\,g$ ($\theta \approx 0.14$, $\phi \approx 0.40$, yielding $D_p/D_o \approx 0.086 > 0.02$)\cite{ming1989lunar,heinse2007porous,podolskiy2005capillary,jones2012beyond}, supporting continuous diffusive oxygen transport to root cell boundaries and preventing hypoxia without requiring deep regolith excavation\cite{hasenstein2026assessing,stout2001evidence}.

\subsection{Dynamic Root-Intrusion Drift and Water Control Trade-Offs}
\label{subsec:control_tradeoffs}

A critical operational vulnerability highlighted by Jones et al. (2012) is that plant root growth and microbial biofilm accumulation dynamically alter porous-medium hydraulic properties over a single crop cycle\cite{jones2012beyond}. In a 42-day dwarf rice (\textit{Oryza sativa} L.) experiment within a confined $1000\,\mathrm{cm}^3$ calcined-clay (Profile) root zone, root intrusion displaced $\sim 10\text{--}11\%$ of total pore space ($17\%$ of inter-aggregate macropores), shifting the fitted van Genuchten parameters from $\alpha = 0.086\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}, n = 5.1, \theta_s = 0.71$ to $\alpha = 0.055\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}, n = 3.1, \theta_s = 0.60$ (Table~\ref{tab:van_genuchten_jones})\cite{jones2012beyond}.

This temporal hydraulic drift complicates both conventional spaceflight water-control strategies\cite{jones2012beyond}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Matric Potential (Suction) Control:} Maintaining a fixed negative-pressure set point (e.g., $-8\,\mathrm{cm}$ head) via porous tubes or tensiometers provides gravity-independent transportability across $0\,g$, $0.16\,g$, and $0.38\,g$\cite{jones2012beyond}. However, leftward-shifting water retention curves during root intrusion reduce both volumetric water content and unsaturated hydraulic conductivity at the fixed suction set point, while powered negative-pressure lines remain vulnerable to crop loss during power outages\cite{jones2012beyond}.
    \item \textbf{Volumetric Water Content (VWC) Control:} Sensor-based VWC regulation (e.g., HYDRUS-1D simulations of $15\,\mathrm{cm}$ lunar regolith beds maintaining $>25\%$ air-filled porosity regardless of $g$)\cite{jones2012beyond} can stabilize fine-textured media, but historical heat-pulse probes (such as on the Mir SVET greenhouse) suffer from contact-resistance drift as roots invade, while electromagnetic time-domain reflectometry (TDR) and capacitance sensors face spacecraft EMI/safety restrictions\cite{jones2012beyond}.
\end{itemize}
Sintered regolith bio-infrastructure directly resolves this multi-cycle drift: because the vitrified aluminosilicate matrix withstands cyclic $500^\circ\mathrm{C}$ pyrolytic oxidation between harvests (Section~\ref{subsec:thermal_regeneration}), accumulated root biomass and biofilms are completely mineralized, resetting $\alpha$, $n$, and $\theta_s$ back to their calibrated baseline values before every planting cycle.

\subsection{Passive Capillary Fluidics: Insights from ISS Plant Water Management (PWM-5 and PWM-6)}
\label{subsec:pwm_capillary_fluidics}

A complementary strategy to packed porous beds is passive, geometry-driven capillary fluidics, recently demonstrated aboard the ISS in NASA's Plant Water Management (PWM-5 and PWM-6) investigations (SpaceX-29)\cite{nasa_pwm,weislogel2026pwm,hatch2022plant}. Rather than relying on gravity or mechanical phase separators, PWM-5 and PWM-6 utilize open-wedge channels, passive bubble separators and diverters, and serial/parallel network geometries to drive recirculating hydroponic and ebb-and-flow watering cycles entirely through surface tension, wetting, and channel geometry\cite{weislogel2026pwm,monje2026hydroponic}. Tests incorporating 3D-printed root analogs across multiple growth densities demonstrated that plant roots in unconfined wedge channels act as capillary phase separators themselves—stabilizing gas--liquid interfaces and enabling passive bubbly-flow aeration without the $10\text{--}17\%$ inter-aggregate pore collapse observed in confined porous substrates\cite{weislogel2026pwm,monje2026hydroponic,jones2012beyond}.

For lunar bio-infrastructure, PWM-5/6 physics and sintered regolith manufacturing are directly synergistic. Using SLM or concentrated solar sintering (Section~\ref{sec:manufacturing}), ISRU facilities can fabricate hydrophilic, vitrified open-wedge PWM troughs and passive bubble diverters directly from lunar dust, paired with $30\text{--}60\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ sintered porous wicking pins for seed germination and ebb-and-flow priming\cite{ming1989lunar,weislogel2026pwm,hasenstein2026assessing}. This dual-scale capillary architecture preserves passive, pump-free aeration during mature root expansion while eliminating the launch mass of Earth-imported polymeric fluidics.
